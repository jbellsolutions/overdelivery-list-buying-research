# The Over Delivery List-Buying Guide

## Executive thesis

The Brian Kurtz / *Over Delivery* tradition is not “find the biggest database and mail it.” It is the discipline of becoming fluent in **who the audience is, what it has bought, what it responds to, how the owner protects the relationship, and how every test compounds into proprietary knowledge**.

A useful buy therefore has five parts:

1. **Audience truth** — source, behavior, recency, frequency, value, channel, and permission.
2. **Congruence** — the offer fits what the audience expects from the publisher/list owner.
3. **Controlled test** — a small, measurable test with a pre-agreed readout.
4. **Learning loop** — results feed segmentation, creative, product, and house-file decisions.
5. **Relationship protection** — no short-term rental fee justifies burning a high-value audience.

Kurtz’s archive reinforces this repeatedly: Boardroom’s list quality mattered because it consisted of buyers/subscribers; Phillips Publishing asked uncomfortable questions before mailing; list managers had to protect the relationship even when offered large checks; and the house file was used to develop products, not merely to blast offers.

## The market map

### 1. List owners and publishers

They own or control an audience relationship: paid newsletters, magazines, associations, vertical publishers, buyer files, donor files, membership organizations, and creator newsletters. This is the closest modern equivalent to the classic direct-response list owner.

**Buy:** named-list rental, dedicated email, newsletter sponsorship, insert, direct mail, co-registration, or negotiated media package.

**What to demand:** media kit, source/acquisition story, buyer/subscriber status, recency/frequency/value selects, test history, list hygiene, suppression, offer-clearance rules, and current reporting.

### 2. List brokers and managers

Brokers represent multiple owners and help with selection, clearance, counts, formats, merge/purge, and scheduling. Managers represent specific lists and protect the owner’s relationship.

**Buy:** intelligence and access—not just names. A broker who cannot explain source, selection history, or congruence is a routing service, not a strategic list partner.

### 3. List/media discovery databases

These are discovery and planning layers. They help find programs, channels, providers, and contact paths. They are not proof that every listed audience is available or good.

**Current official example:** NextMark’s Media Finder says it searches 100,000+ media programs across postal lists, telephone, package inserts, email, SMS, and display. Its live pricing page exposes shared-minute plans of $159/$318/$531/$706 per month for 100/250/500/1,000 minutes and unlimited plans from $495/month for one user to $3,167/month for 20 users. That is the platform subscription—not a guarantee of list-rental economics. Use it to build the candidate universe, then clear each source individually.

### 4. Newsletter sponsorship marketplaces

These aggregate or facilitate publisher inventory. They are the modern, measurable bridge between list-owner thinking and email media buying.

**Current official examples:**

- **Paved** — official advertiser material describes 3,000+ premium audiences, direct partnerships, dedicated placements, native/PPC formats, audience data, reporting, bot filtering, and no subscription fee/no minimum spend. Its terms explicitly say publisher list descriptions are not performance guarantees and require tracking codes.
- **Swapstack** — official material describes newsletter sponsorship at scale and publisher access. Its pricing page says publishers can join free, managed service takes a cut, and an advertiser affiliate listing is $50/month with a one-month free trial plus reporting/tracking.

### 5. Publisher-owned newsletter and media inventory

The second research batch adds a separate publisher-owner layer. These organizations sell sponsorships, dedicated sends, branded content, lead generation, or media packages; they do **not** thereby offer raw subscriber-address rental.

**B2B and operator audiences:** SmartBrief, Search Engine Journal, B2B Marketing, Marketing Dive, CIO Dive, Retail Dive, HR Dive, Healthcare Dive, Restaurant Dive, Chief Marketer, and Digiday. SmartBrief and Search Engine Journal expose the clearest dedicated-email and newsletter mechanics. The six Industry Dive pages were browser-verified after direct HTTP 403s; their current audience and media-kit sales claims are captured, while exact formats, pricing, minimums, consent, and list-transfer rights remain gated.

**Consumer, finance, and enthusiast audiences:** Morning Brew, The Daily Upside, theSkimm, New York Times Advertising, Hearst Magazines, The Atlantic, Outside, FLYING, Farm and Dairy, NRA Publications, NRA Family, Healthline Media, and People Inc. The strongest direct-response signals were qualified-lead language at FLYING, action-oriented audience language at theSkimm, native partner campaigns at Morning Brew, and Healthline’s explicit affiliate/digital advertising products with a $25,000 minimum investment.

Buy these as publisher media—not as list transfers. Require the media kit, audience geography, paid/free subscriber mix, placement format, deliverability reporting, ad labeling, make-good policy, and exact advertiser/sender responsibility before launch.

### 6. Newsletter sponsorship marketplaces and adjacent platforms

The second batch verified additional marketplace mechanics:

- **beehiiv Ad Network** — networked sponsored links with publisher acceptance and verified-click/open mechanics.
- **LetterWell** — self-serve discovery, Instant Booking, publisher-set pricing, and escrow-style campaign handling.
- **Hecto** — creator/newsletter marketplace with checkout and a published 5% platform fee.
- **Passionfroot** — broader creator sponsorship workflow with newsletter inventory.
- **SponsorGap** — sponsor discovery/outreach and inbound sponsorship listings; discovery is not guaranteed inventory.
- **KreatorStack** — private-beta newsletter marketplace; monitor, do not assume mature verification or payment protection.
- **LiveIntent and Jeeng** — managed email monetization/advertising networks, not transparent self-serve list marketplaces; detailed policy pages require manual recheck in this environment.
- **SparkLoop** — newsletter recommendation/referral acquisition, useful for growing a first-party audience but not a list-rental product.

### 7. Affiliate networks and partner platforms

These connect offers and partners, but do not give the advertiser ownership of partner audiences. The skeptical verification pass confirmed that impact.com, PartnerStack, Everflow, Awin, CJ, TUNE, ClickBank, MaxBounty, Perform[cb], and FlexOffers are affiliate, partnership, performance, or tracking/managed-acquisition systems—not raw list vendors. MaxBounty now states that it is part of mrge. Worldata currently routes to Outcome Media, and MeritDirect is represented in the current evidence as Anteriad.

### 8. Discovery and relationship channels

MailCon, Affiliate Summit, LeadsCon, Email Innovations World, ANA, DMAW, and SRDS/NextMark are relationship and research infrastructure. Use them to find owners, publishers, brokers, data providers, and media buyers. None is a substitute for current datacards, consent evidence, or controlled testing.

### 9. Solo-ad marketplaces

A solo ad is a dedicated email from someone else’s list to promote the buyer’s offer. It can be useful, but the apparent simplicity hides the core problem: the buyer often knows less about the audience and sender relationship than in classic list brokerage.

**Current official example:** **Udimi** defines solo ads as renting another person’s subscriber list, lists seller profiles, ID verification, ratings/reviews, niche categories, and price-per-click offers. That makes it a practical testing marketplace, not automatic proof of buyer quality or permission.

### 10. Affiliate networks and partner platforms

These connect offers and partners, track referrals, and pay commissions. They may provide email/media partners, but they are not automatically list brokers.

**Current platform examples:** impact.com, PartnerStack, Everflow, Awin, CJ, TUNE, ClickBank, MaxBounty, and Perform[cb]. Their official pages support partnership, publisher, affiliate, creator, tracking, and/or performance-marketing roles. Treat them as **partner-distribution and measurement infrastructure** unless the specific partner and email inventory are independently cleared.

### 11. Data and audience vendors

Data/list providers may offer B2B or consumer records, modeled audiences, custom segments, email deployment, hygiene, or direct-mail files. That is a different product from a permissioned publisher send.

**Current official example:** ListGiant’s official pages expose B2B/consumer categories, data leads, direct-mail lists, custom audiences, email lists, hygiene, and deployment. Its marketing claims require source, permission, freshness, sample, and suppression review. A purchased email file is not automatically permission to send.

## Recommended research stack

### Phase 1: build the universe

1. Start with NextMark and trade/association discovery.
2. Add publisher media kits and sponsorship marketplaces: Paved, Swapstack, and direct publisher sales pages.
3. Add solo marketplaces only as a separate lane: Udimi and comparable marketplaces.
4. Add affiliate networks when the offer supports CPA/rev-share and partner recruitment.
5. Add data vendors only when the use case is direct mail, modeling, append/hygiene, or compliant deployment—not because a large “email database” sounds like a list.

### Phase 2: classify every source

For each source, record:

- owner / broker / manager / marketplace / network / data vendor;
- channel: postal, email, newsletter sponsorship, solo, package insert, affiliate, paid social, display;
- audience: buyer, paid subscriber, free subscriber, lead, clicker, modeled, unknown;
- monetization: rental, CPM, CPC, CPA, rev-share, flat fee, subscription, data license;
- source and permission evidence;
- segment fields and behavioral fields;
- test minimum and reporting;
- complaint/suppression process;
- offer restrictions;
- current URL and date checked;
- confidence: high, medium, low, or historical.

### Phase 3: clear the offer

Send the owner/broker a one-page clearance package:

- product and landing page;
- price and guarantee/refund policy;
- claims and proof;
- target segment;
- creative sample;
- tracking URL and attribution window;
- expected volume and test budget;
- suppression and customer-service contact;
- countries/states targeted.

Ask the owner to describe **why the offer fits**. The quality of that conversation is data.

### Phase 4: test like a direct marketer

Use a test cell large enough to produce a directional read but small enough to protect cash and the relationship. Predefine:

- cost basis: CPM/CPC/flat fee/CPA;
- delivered, unique, bot-filtered clicks, leads, sales, refunds, complaints, unsubscribes;
- contribution margin—not gross revenue alone;
- attribution window and deduplication;
- acceptable complaint/refund ceiling;
- scale decision and next segment.

Do not judge one source on opens alone. Do not scale a source because a vendor promises “buyers.” Do not let a platform’s tracking dashboard substitute for source intelligence.

### Solo-ad risk ladder

- **Udimi:** strongest transaction-protection structure reviewed, with seller identity checks, platform payment/delivery controls, ratings, and disputes; still requires consent and source-quality diligence.
- **10DollarSoloAds / EasySoloAds:** concrete low-cost packages and tracking claims, but restrictive refund terms after campaign start and unnamed underlying lists.
- **SoloAdsX:** discovery directory, not escrow or standardized platform underwriting.
- **My Lead Gen Secret:** permission-based lead-distribution model, not equivalent to a named-list solo send.
- **TrafficForMe:** high-risk candidate because strong sales-page claims conflicted with the independent review signal captured in the research; do not scale without a small test and enforceable refund terms.

“Prospects reached,” delivered clicks, unique clicks, opt-ins, and sales are separate metrics. Every solo-ad test gets its own landing page, UTM/campaign ID, first-party analytics, and written delivery/refund terms.

### Affiliate-network distinctions

- **ClickBank:** strongest direct-response/email-marketer fit reviewed, but offer-level email approval is still required.
- **Awin, CJ, impact.com:** broad publisher and brand discovery networks.
- **PartnerStack:** strongest B2B SaaS partner ecosystem.
- **Everflow and TUNE:** tracking/partner-management infrastructure, not turnkey compliant publisher inventory.
- **Gen3 Marketing and Acceleration Partners:** hands-on agencies, not open self-serve marketplaces.

No reviewed network granted universal email approval. Written approval must name the exact offer, sender/list, geography, frequency, creative, tracking method, and suppression process.

### Discovery infrastructure

Use **ANA, DMAW, DMA UK, FEDMA, NEMOA, IAB, MediaPost, NextMark, and SRDS** to find active practitioners, owners, managers, publishers, events, datacards, and warm introductions. Treat association memberships, event rosters, and publication audiences as discovery infrastructure—not as proof of rentable list inventory.

### Compliance gate

The completed compliance lane added these non-negotiables:

- seller “opt-in” language is not consent evidence;
- require exact signup wording, source, timestamp, channel, frequency, advertiser/partner disclosure, and suppression history;
- outsourcing delivery does not transfer CAN-SPAM responsibility;
- UK PECR and GDPR require specific, demonstrable consent or another documented lawful basis;
- CCPA/CPRA sale/share opt-outs are separate from email unsubscribe handling;
- Google recommends spam rates below 0.10% and avoiding 0.30%+; Yahoo requires bulk senders to remain below 0.3% and honor unsubscribes within two days;
- Mailchimp prohibits rented, purchased, third-party, co-registration, and partner lists;
- contracts should define bounce/complaint ceilings, pause rules, remediation, suppression, make-goods, and refund rights.

These controls belong in the buying process before the first send, not after a deliverability incident.

### Postal list rental

Best when the offer has a strong long-form story, higher AOV, continuity, book/newsletter/product economics, or a proven direct-mail backend.

Start with named buyer/subscriber lists and rental selections. Ask for continuation history, hotline/controls, source, recency, and modeled selects. Use merge/purge and test cells. Protect the owner’s relationship with conservative clearance and customer-service readiness.

### Dedicated email / solo

Best when the offer is simple to understand, fast to measure, and capable of turning a click into a lead or sale without requiring a long nurture sequence.

Require sender identity, permission basis, dedicated-send rules, seed addresses, click definition, complaint/unsubscribe handling, and suppression. Start with one seller/publisher and one creative. Never upload a rented file into your own ESP without explicit permission and legal review.

### Newsletter sponsorship

Best when the offer benefits from trust, editorial context, and repeated exposure. Buy direct or through Paved/Swapstack, but compare publisher-level performance—not marketplace averages.

Use a placement matrix: audience fit, paid/free subscriber mix, frequency, ad position, copy limits, dedicated-vs-native format, tracking, and historic conversion evidence.

### Affiliate / partner distribution

Best when you can support rev-share, tracking, creative, customer service, refunds, and partner enablement. The partner’s audience and promotional method matter more than the network brand.

Clear email, incentivized traffic, paid search, brand bidding, co-registration, and sub-affiliate rules before launch. Pay for outcomes only when source quality and attribution are observable.

## The RFM layer: the non-negotiable data request

The lost-chapters archive makes the framework explicit:

- **R — recency:** recent buyers/inquirers (“hotlines”) generally command a premium and often determine whether a list pays out.
- **F — frequency:** combine recent buyers with multibuyers; frequency can outweigh recency when previous customers have high relationship value.
- **M — monetary value:** tier customers by cumulative spend, then combine monetary value with recency and frequency.

This gives the buyer a better question than “How big is the list?” Ask: **Can this source select by buyer status, recency, frequency, and value—or is it just a reach number?**

For a rented source, request the closest available analogues: paid vs free subscriber, purchase count, last purchase, category purchased, order value band, renewal/expiry, click depth, and engagement recency. If the source cannot provide the fields, lower the fit score and compensate with a smaller test.

## The “master of lists” operating system

1. Keep a living **source ledger**, not a vendor bookmark folder.
2. Record every test at source + segment + creative + date level.
3. Separate **audience access** from **measurement infrastructure**.
4. Build a relationship map: owner, manager, broker, clearance contact, operations contact, and complaint contact.
5. Build a response library: what each source bought, clicked, ignored, complained about, refunded, or renewed.
6. Re-test winners with new creative and adjacent segments; do not assume a list is permanently “good.”
7. Develop products from repeated audience signals, as Boardroom did with newsletter and house-file knowledge.
8. Treat first-party opt-ins and customers as the compounding asset. The rented source is a learning and acquisition channel.

## What “reputable” should mean here

For this repository, reputable means **evidence quality and operating discipline**, not an unverified popularity ranking. A provider earns a high-confidence classification when its current official material clearly describes its role, mechanics, audience/inventory relationship, and controls, and when its claims are not stretched beyond the evidence.

A high-confidence platform can still be a bad fit for a particular offer. Conversely, a smaller owner with transparent source history, strong congruence, and clean test reporting may be more valuable than a large network.
